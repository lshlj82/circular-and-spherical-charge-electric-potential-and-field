The long journey to the ring

Release a charge of the opposite sign from rest and ask how long it takes to reach the ring. Exactly on the axis it never arrives. Just off the axis, the answer depends on the release height: near the center the sideways offset grows, but from high enough on the axis the charge spends most of each swing where the sideways force pulls it back, and it stays near the axis indefinitely. Everywhere else it slingshots around the wire, and whether it ever hits depends on how thick the wire is.

Why the potentials match

Potential is a scalar sum of kedq/s. When every charge element sits at the same distance R, only the total charge matters, so any such arrangement gives keq/R. The field is a gradient, so it depends on how V changes nearby, which one value cannot tell you.

The ring's saddle

Near the ring's center, V ≈ (keq/R)(1 + (x² + y² − 2z²)/4R²). The bold contour crosses itself at the center in the side slice. A test charge is pushed back along one set of directions and away along the other, so without a constraint it always escapes.

The shell's flat interior

Inside the shell V is constant and E is zero everywhere. Patch area grows as s², exactly offsetting the 1/s² field, so opposite patches cancel. A test charge inside feels no force at all: neutral equilibrium.

Made by Claude Opus 5.5. The ring's potential and field are computed from complete elliptic integrals; test-charge motion uses a fourth-order Runge–Kutta integrator. The derivations are in the accompanying note (PDF).
